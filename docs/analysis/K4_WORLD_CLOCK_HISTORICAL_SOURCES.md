## Finding in brief

The missing historical source is no longer a dead end: several public catalogue records document photographs of the Weltzeituhr from 1969–1980s, and one freely viewable, high-resolution photograph dates to 31 January 1970. An image catalogue also lists a high-resolution Erhard Pansegrau photograph dated approximately 1986/1990. These sources can support a future label-by-label transcription.

They do **not**, by themselves, establish the complete face-by-face layout as it stood in 1990. This document records the source leads and the constraints they establish without inventing unreadable labels or treating an inferred DDR-era list as observed fact.

## What can be stated safely

1. The clock was inaugurated on 30 September 1969. The 1970 Commons image is therefore a near-original-era view, not a modern reconstruction.
2. The official history states that the original clock had **80 city names** across the 24 time zones; a 2019 interview/caption associated with designer Erich John says **97 originally mentioned cities** and 148 after additions. The current official description lists 146. These are conflicting counts, potentially using different counting conventions; none should be silently selected as a verified plate inventory.
3. A 1997 restoration changed some names and corrected some zone assignments. At minimum, the commonly reported changes include Leningrad/St. Petersburg and Alma-Ata/Almaty.
4. The 2017 transcription and an inferred “DDR-era” city list are different kinds of evidence. The former is a transcription of a later visible state; the latter is a reconstruction unless supported label-by-label by period photographs or a primary record.
5. The hour ring rotates; the cited construction/history descriptions do not establish that the city-name plates themselves rotate through a reading position. A cipher hypothesis must specify its spatial traversal rather than assuming a rotating city drum.

## Important count discrepancy

The current official site says the clock originally had 80 city names. A 2019 B.Z. interview/caption associated with designer Erich John instead says 97 cities were originally mentioned and 148 after additions. The official site now lists 146. Separately, contemporaneous 1997 reports describe 20 names being added. These figures do not reconcile into a simple “original + 20 = current” change model; the original count itself is disputed across sources.

Do not infer a complete original list by subtracting a short list of politically sensitive cities from the 2017 transcription. The mismatch may reflect changes across multiple periods, different definitions/counting conventions, or incomplete secondary summaries. A dated primary source or direct plate-by-plate transcription is needed to resolve it.

## Transcription protocol for the next pass

When a source image is acquired and examined, record each candidate label with:

- image/catalogue identifier, URL, date and photographer/rights holder;
- face or zone identifier only when it can be grounded in visible geometry;
- plate side (upper/lower), exact visible spelling, and whether punctuation/diacritics are visible;
- confidence (confirmed, probable, unreadable) and a short reason;
- whether the text is directly visible in that image or inferred from a different source;
- a crop reference or image coordinates so a second researcher can repeat the reading.

Do not silently normalize historical spellings, move a city to another zone, fill unreadable text from a modern list, or treat an empty visible plate as a confirmed blank. Keep observed text and normalized search keys in separate fields.

## Recommended next actions

1. Inspect the 1970 Commons original at full resolution and create a partial transcription only for labels that can actually be read.
2. Ask the Verein für die Geschichte Berlins archive about Mi-A 245 (1980s slide) and Mi-A I615 (1976 full-frame view); ask whether the catalogued scans show the city plates sharply enough for transcription.
3. Ask AKG about licensed research access to Pansegrau's ca. 1986/1990 lossless image. This is the highest-priority near-period candidate, but its approximate date must be retained.
4. Search the official designer / restoration records for an original 1969 label schedule or 1997 replacement list. A change log could resolve the 80-versus-current-count discrepancy more reliably than photo inference.
5. Only after a label table is independently checked should the project generate explicit per-letter streams. Declare face start, traversal direction, top/bottom selection, spelling normalization and treatment of unknowns before testing the cribs.
