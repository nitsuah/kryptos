## Finding in brief

The missing historical source is no longer a dead end: several public catalogue records document photographs of the Weltzeituhr from 1969–1980s, a freely viewable 4,955 × 5,000 px Fortepan image dates to 1970, and two Federal Archives records are explicitly dated 24 April and 30 September 1990. An image catalogue also lists a high-resolution Erhard Pansegrau photograph dated approximately 1986/1990. These sources can support a future label-by-label transcription.

They do **not**, by themselves, establish the complete face-by-face layout as it stood in 1990. This document records the source leads and the constraints they establish without inventing unreadable labels or treating an inferred DDR-era list as observed fact.

## What can be stated safely

1. The clock was inaugurated on 30 September 1969. The 1970 Commons image is therefore a near-original-era view, not a modern reconstruction.
2. The original clock was a multi-party build, not simply a one-firm fabrication: Erich John was the designer; the Berlin art-historical catalogue credits Hans-Joachim Kunsch as metalworker and notes the participation of two Rathenow firms. That catalogue does not assign individual components or plate/lettering work to specific participants. Separately, the 2024 workshop report says Kunsch Metallbau retains construction documents and original lettering samples, making the company a high-priority archive contact. Seek primary drawings and schedules rather than infer component assignments from the catalogue.
3. The official history states that the original clock had **80 city names** across the 24 time zones; a 2019 B.Z. interview/caption associated with designer Erich John says **97 originally mentioned cities** and 148 after additions; a 2019 Tagesspiegel caption says 147; the current official description lists 146. These are conflicting counts, potentially using different counting conventions and reflecting multiple rounds of change (including reported 1985 engraving/re-engraving and 1997 changes); none should be silently selected as a verified plate inventory.
4. A 1997 restoration changed some names and corrected some zone assignments. At minimum, the commonly reported changes include Leningrad/St. Petersburg and Alma-Ata/Almaty.
5. The 2017 transcription and an inferred “DDR-era” city list are different kinds of evidence. The former is a transcription of a later visible state; the latter is a reconstruction unless supported label-by-label by period photographs or a primary record.
6. The hour ring rotates; the cited construction/history descriptions do not establish that the city-name plates themselves rotate through a reading position. A cipher hypothesis must specify its spatial traversal rather than assuming a rotating city drum.

## Important count discrepancy

The current official site says the clock originally had 80 city names. A 2019 B.Z. interview/caption associated with designer Erich John instead says 97 cities were originally mentioned and 148 after additions. The official site now lists 146. Separately, contemporaneous 1997 reports describe 20 names being added. These figures do not reconcile into a simple “original + 20 = current” change model; the original count itself is disputed across sources.

Do not infer a complete original list by subtracting a short list of politically sensitive cities from the 2017 transcription. The mismatch may reflect changes across multiple periods, different definitions/counting conventions, or incomplete secondary summaries. A dated primary source or direct plate-by-plate transcription is needed to resolve it.

## Additional reported changes at the 1985 maintenance (secondary-source lead)

A 2019 Tagesspiegel report says that during the first major maintenance in 1985, **Athens, Copenhagen, Vienna, and Rome were engraved**; the same article says **Istanbul was added in 1997 at the request of the governing mayor**. This is significant because the common summary of the 1997 restoration is not the only reported maintenance period. The article describes engraving/re-engraving, not necessarily additions to the city inventory, so it should not be used to infer a count delta. City counts still cannot be reconciled by simply adding the 20 names reported in December 1997 to the official original count of 80.

Source: [Der Tagesspiegel, 14 April 2019](https://www.tagesspiegel.de/berlin/die-weltzeituhr-kennt-jeder-niemand-ihren-erfinder-6541270.html), especially the section “1997 kam Istanbul hinzu.” The article is a retrospective account, not a contemporaneous 1985 work order; treat these as leads to verify against dated photographs and the production/restoration files.

These examples prove that the restoration included additions, renamings and time-zone corrections. They do **not** explain the numerical difference between 80 original names, 97 originally mentioned names, 146 current names, and the reported 20 additions. Do not treat these examples as a complete diff.

## Transcription protocol for the next pass

When a source image is acquired and examined, record each candidate label with:

- image/catalogue identifier, URL, date and photographer/rights holder;
- face or zone identifier only when it can be grounded in visible geometry;
- plate side (upper/lower), exact visible spelling, and whether punctuation/diacritics are visible;
- confidence (confirmed, probable, unreadable) and a short reason;
- whether the text is directly visible in that image or inferred from a different source;
- a crop reference or image coordinates so a second researcher can repeat the reading.

Do not silently normalize historical spellings, move a city to another zone, fill unreadable text from a modern list, or treat an empty visible plate as a confirmed blank. Keep observed text and normalized search keys in separate fields.

1. Inspect the [Fortepan 60040 original](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kelet-Berlin,_Alexanderplatz._Fortepan_60040.jpg) at full resolution and create a partial 1970 transcription only for labels that can actually be read. Preserve crop coordinates and mark obscured panels unreadable.
2. Resolve the provenance conflict in the [captioned 1991 Kaufhof photo](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Berlin_Alexanderplatz_Kaufhof.jpg): the preview seems to show labels that contemporary 1997 reporting describes as newly added/renamed. Verify original source and date before treating it as a 1991 plate record.
3. Obtain and inspect Hans-Werner Klünner and Gerhard Ullmann, *Straßenmöbel in Berlin* (1983), the catalogue cited by Berlin's monument database; its section on public clocks is catalogued at pp. 38–45. Check library copies or ask the relevant Berlin archive whether the pages can be consulted or scanned.
4. **Prioritize Kunsch Metallbau’s surviving 1969 construction records.** A 23 Nov 2024 B.Z. workshop report quotes Stefan Kunsch saying the firm still has all necessary construction documents and original lettering samples for the clock. The firm's [official contact page](https://kunsch-metallbau.de/impressum/) lists **info@kunsch-metallbau.de**, **+49 30 557 81 67**, and Siegfriedstraße 171, 10365 Berlin; its [2025 repair reference](https://kunsch-metallbau.de/referenzen-/weltzeituhr-berlin-alexanderplatz-reparatur-platten/) confirms recent work on two World Clock inscription and lower plates. Ask specifically for an original city-name/plate schedule, fabrication drawings showing plate order, and any 1997 restoration replacement list. The sources establish a direct contact and surviving construction records, not that a complete city schedule survives. No contact has been made.
5. Request access to the primary documentation identified in a 2009 study bibliography as “Privatarchiv Prof. em. Erich John – Dokumentation Urania-Weltzeituhr.” The official [Weltzeituhr Berlin contact form](https://www.weltzeituhr-berlin.de/en) explicitly invites questions about the clock's history and creator; its background page says Erich John personally entrusted the company with commercial rights and the duty to communicate the clock's history. Ask whether its historical documentation or the designer's archive contains the original city-name schedule, plate layout, and 1997 change list, or can direct researchers to the archive's current custodian. No outreach has been sent. These records could resolve the 80-versus-current-count discrepancy without inference.
6. Ask the Verein für die Geschichte Berlins archive about Mi-A 245 (1980s slide) and Mi-A I615 (1976 full-frame view); ask whether the original scans show the city plates sharply enough for transcription.
7. Ask AKG about licensed research access to Pansegrau's ca. 1986/1990 lossless image. It remains the highest-priority near-period image candidate, but its approximate catalogue date must be retained.
8. Use the two dated 1990 Bundesarchiv images for contextual cross-checks only; their current 785 × 537 and 784 × 532 px versions are not sharp enough for reliable label transcription.
9. Only after a label table is independently checked should the project generate explicit per-letter streams. Declare face start, traversal direction, top/bottom selection, spelling normalization and treatment of unknowns before testing the cribs.
